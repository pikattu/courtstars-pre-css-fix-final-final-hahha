How the Game Works

The Core Rules

⏱️
Game Structure

An NBA game consists of four 12-minute quarters (48 minutes total). If tied after regulation, 5-minute overtime periods are played until a winner is decided. Teams switch which basket they shoot at after halftime.

Structure
🧑‍🤝‍🧑
Teams & Rosters

Each team plays 5 players on the court at a time. NBA rosters hold up to 15 players (13 active per game). There are no set positions in the rules — teams choose their own lineup strategy.

Roster
⏰
Shot Clock

The offensive team must attempt a shot that hits the rim within 24 seconds of gaining possession. If they fail, it's a "shot clock violation" and the other team gets the ball. This prevents stalling and keeps the pace high.

Timing
🏃
Dribbling

Players must bounce (dribble) the ball while moving. Once a player stops dribbling and holds the ball with both hands, they may no longer dribble again — they must pass or shoot. Resuming a dribble is called "double dribbling" and results in a turnover.

Movement
👣
Traveling

A player cannot move both feet without dribbling. Players are allowed two steps after gathering the ball (the "gather step" rule in the NBA), but taking additional steps without dribbling is called traveling — the ball is given to the other team.

Movement
🔄
Half-Court & Backcourt

Once the offense advances the ball past the half-court line, they cannot return to their own backcourt. Doing so is a backcourt violation, resulting in a turnover. The offense has 8 seconds to cross half-court after gaining possession.

Court Rules
🎯
Out of Bounds

The ball is "out of bounds" if it crosses the sidelines or baseline. The last player to touch it before it goes out loses possession. Players can step out of bounds to catch a pass, but must re-establish themselves inbounds before touching the ball.

Court Rules
📐
The Key (Paint Area)

The rectangular area under each basket is called "the paint" or "the key." An offensive player may not stand in the paint for more than 3 seconds at a time (3-second violation). Defensive players are also tracked, though the rules differ slightly.

02
Points on the Board

How Scoring Works

1
Free Throw

Awarded after certain fouls. The shooter takes uncontested shots from the free throw line (15 ft from the basket).

2
Field Goal

Any made basket from inside the three-point arc — layups, mid-range jumpers, dunks, and floaters all count as 2 points.

3
Three-Pointer

Any made shot with the shooter's feet behind the three-point arc at the time of release. The arc is 23 ft 9 in from the basket in the NBA.

+1
And-One

When a player is fouled while making their shot, the basket counts and they receive one additional free throw attempt — colloquially called an "and-one."

03
On the Court

The Five Positions

1
PG
Point Guard

The floor general. Runs the offense, controls the pace, and distributes the ball. Usually the best passer and ball-handler on the team.

e.g. Steph Curry, Luka Doncic
2
SG
Shooting Guard

A perimeter scorer and off-ball threat. Often the team's best three-point shooter. Also covers opposing guards on defense.

e.g. Klay Thompson, Devin Booker
3
SF
Small Forward

A versatile wing who can score, defend multiple positions, and operate at every level of the court. The NBA's most prized position type today.

e.g. LeBron James, Jayson Tatum
4
PF
Power Forward

A physical presence near the basket who rebounds, scores in the paint, and sets screens. Modern PFs are also expected to shoot threes.

e.g. Giannis, Anthony Davis
5
C
Center

The tallest player, anchoring the defense near the rim, protecting the basket, and finishing around the basket on offense. Also called the "big man."

e.g. Nikola Jokic, Joel Embiid

Reading the Box Score

Stat Acronyms Decoded

PPG
Points Per Game
The average number of points a player scores each game over a season. The primary offensive statistic.
League avg ≈ 25 PPG for All-Stars; 30+ PPG is elite
RPG
Rebounds Per Game
Average number of missed shot recoveries per game. Split into OREB (offensive) and DREB (defensive).
10+ RPG is considered dominant
APG
Assists Per Game
Average passes per game that directly lead to a made basket. Measures playmaking and court vision.
7+ APG marks a true playmaking point guard
SPG
Steals Per Game
Average number of times per game a defensive player legally takes the ball from the offense.
2+ SPG is considered elite at any position
BPG
Blocks Per Game
Average shot rejections per game — when a defender legally deflects a shot attempt by the offense.
3+ BPG defines a premier shot-blocker
MPG
Minutes Per Game
Average playing time per game. Starters typically log 30–38 minutes; key reserves 15–25 minutes.
36+ MPG indicates a team's cornerstone
FG%
Field Goal Percentage
Ratio of made shots to total attempts from the field (excluding free throws). Shows general shooting efficiency.
50%+ is highly efficient; avg is ~46%
3P%
Three-Point Percentage
Percentage of three-point attempts made. Critical in today's spacing-dominated NBA.
37%+ is average; 40%+ is excellent on volume
FT%
Free Throw Percentage
Percentage of uncontested free throws made. A pure shooting skill test — no defenders, no time pressure.
80%+ is very good; 90%+ is elite
TS%
True Shooting Percentage
An advanced stat combining FG%, 3P%, and FT% to give a holistic view of shooting efficiency per possession.
Formula: Pts ÷ (2 × TSA) where TSA = FGA + 0.44 × FTA
PER
Player Efficiency Rating
Created by John Hollinger. A single number summarizing per-minute statistical accomplishments. The league average is always set to 15.0.
20+ is All-Star level; 25+ is MVP territory
+/-
Plus / Minus
The score differential when a specific player is on the court. A +10 means the team outscored opponents by 10 points while that player played.
Positive = team performs better with you; negative = worse
TOV
Turnovers
When the offensive team loses ball possession to the defense — through bad passes, out-of-bounds, violations, or steals.
Under 2.5 per game is the target for most players
DD / TD
Double-Double / Triple-Double
Reaching double figures (10+) in two statistical categories in a single game is a "double-double." Doing it in three categories (pts/reb/ast) is a "triple-double."
Triple-doubles signal elite all-around impact
05
Infractions & Penalties

Fouls & Violations

Personal Foul Foul

Illegal physical contact with an opponent — pushing, hitting, holding, or illegal screens. Each player is allowed 6 personal fouls before being ejected ("fouled out"). Depending on game situation, the fouled team may get free throws.

Flagrant Foul Foul

Excessive or unnecessary contact. A Flagrant 1 results in two free throws and possession; a Flagrant 2 results in automatic ejection of the offending player. Reviewed by officials on instant replay.

Technical Foul Technical

Unsportsmanlike conduct — arguing with officials, excessive celebration, leaving the bench, or delay of game. Results in one free throw for the opposing team. Two technicals in a game = automatic ejection.

Traveling Violation

Moving both feet without dribbling. In the NBA, players receive a "gather step" before their two legal steps, but taking extra steps results in a turnover. One of the most called violations at every level.

Double Dribble Violation

Dribbling with both hands simultaneously, or stopping a dribble and then dribbling again. Both result in an immediate turnover — the other team inbounds the ball from the sideline.

Goaltending Violation

Interfering with a shot while the ball is on its downward arc toward the basket, or touching the ball while it is on or directly above the rim. The basket is automatically counted if done by the defense; the shot is waved off if done by the offense.

Shot Clock Violation Violation

Failing to attempt a shot that hits the rim within 24 seconds of gaining possession. The clock resets on any shot that hits the rim. After a violation, the other team inbounds from the sideline near where the violation occurred.

3-Second Violation Violation

An offensive player standing in the paint (free throw lane) for more than 3 consecutive seconds. To reset the count, they must fully exit the paint. Prevents teams from parking a big man under the basket indefinitely.

Charge vs. Block Foul

One of basketball's most contested calls. A "charge" is called when an offensive player runs into a stationary defender (offensive foul). A "block" is called when a defender is still moving. The defender must have their feet set before contact to draw a charge.
